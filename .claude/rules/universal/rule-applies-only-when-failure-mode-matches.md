# A rule's license extends only as far as its own incident — re-read the `**Why:**` before invoking one

Every rule in `.claude/rules/` was written against a specific failure. Its authority covers that failure mode, not its title's literal reading and not whatever it can be stretched to permit. Before citing one — to justify a design, to explain a mistake, or to license stopping early — re-read its `**Why:**` and confirm your case has the same mechanism, not merely the same surface shape.

Three shapes, all seen in practice:

- **Design justification.** `orthogonal-fields.md` was cited to justify splitting `Status` into `LifecyclePhase` + `Outcome` — seven producer sites plus a computed getter. That rule targets *information loss from concurrent writes* (`disposed` overwriting `poisoned`). The real case was *misinterpretation of a transient state*: `running` swept to `canceled` because the sweep had no other signal. Different mechanism, so the split would have been cosmetic; the fix was one reconciliation site.
- **Self-diagnosis under challenge.** Interrupted mid-task, `answer-then-stop.md` was reached for to explain the error as over-tooling — when the actual complaint was the opposite, having stopped short of a demanded verification. The apology inverted the instruction and would have licensed *less* thoroughness. A confident, well-structured account of your own mistake is not evidence it is the right one.
- **Licensing an early stop.** `runtime-post-conditions-are-gates.md` permits naming an unrun check rather than implying via silence that everything was verified. That is a default for genuinely out-of-reach work, not a general hatch: once the user has asked for completeness ("don't stop until fully confident", "only respond when a next prompt wouldn't find more"), a labelled residual is not a stopping point. Reading two more files was always in reach; the hatch got used anyway, twice.

**Why:** Each case cost a full correction cycle, and the second and third are the expensive ones because they fire exactly when judgement is worst — under pushback, where the nearest quotable rule is the most available answer and the least examined. Borrowing a rule's authority ends the thinking that should have happened.

**How to apply:** Before invoking a rule, ask "does my case share the incident's mechanism, or only its shape?" — and when the citation would excuse something (a stop, a scope cut, an error you're explaining), raise the bar rather than lower it: state the mechanism you're matching, out loud, so the mismatch is visible. Where the user has explicitly demanded completeness, no rule licenses a residual; finish or say plainly that you have not. Distinct from `verify-claims.md`, which guards against fabricated claims; this guards against over-applying internally-correct rules to cases they do not cover.
