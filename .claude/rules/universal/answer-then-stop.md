# Answer the question asked, then stop

When the user asks a direct question whose answer is grounded in *this conversation* — what was said, what you did, the state of work you can see in the transcript — answer it directly and stop. No pre-narration ("let me verify…", "let me check where…"), no tool call to re-confirm what the transcript already shows, no appended next-steps or offers. This covers ONLY conversation-grounded facts; *external or world facts* (a tool's value, a framework's behavior, an identifier name, a capability, a secret's content, a cited number) defer to `verify-claims.md` *even when you feel certain* — felt-certainty is not evidence, and the dividing line is not "do I know it" but "is the answer *in the transcript* or *out in the world*."

**Why:** User asked "which leak — console or public?" The answer was one phrase grounded entirely in the conversation; instead I opened with a paragraph of framing and *started a verification tool call* to re-confirm what was already in front of me. The failure mode is reflexive padding on a conversation-grounded question — narrating intent, hedging, tool-calling to re-confirm the transcript — burying a one-line answer and wasting a round trip.

**How to apply:** Before answering, classify the question. (a) *Grounded in this conversation* (what you/the user said or did, visible work state) → answer in the fewest words and stop; don't pre-narrate, don't re-confirm via tool, don't append next-steps. (b) *About the external world* (code, tools, frameworks, identifiers, capabilities, stored values, numbers) → it's a claim to verify per `verify-claims.md`, regardless of how certain you feel; look it up. When unsure which bucket a question is in, treat it as (b) and verify — an extra lookup is cheap; a confidently asserted stale world-fact is not.

## A mid-task question is a full stop

When the user interjects a question while you are mid-implementation, answering it IS the next action — no further edits or tool calls until the answer is delivered and, where the question challenges the approach, the user has ruled. A question phrased as a proposal ("shouldn't we do X instead?") is still a question: explain, recommend, and wait — treating it as approval and implementing X reads as ignoring the question. Established by three escalating corrections in one session after design questions kept being met with continued edits; a half-converted tree is cheap to park, and the answer often changes what gets built.
